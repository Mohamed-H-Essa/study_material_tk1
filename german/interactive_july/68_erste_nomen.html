68 · Das erste Nomen ist nicht immer das Subjekt

The video's rule: in a German main clause the verb is always in position 2, and the subject sits in position 1 or 3. That makes position 1 a free slot — time, place, and (the part English speakers trip over) even the object can go there. So the first noun is not always the doer. You now know every Akkusativ signal — den, einen, meinen, mich, ihn — and the second half of this lesson is reading practice: let the form, not the position, tell you who does it.

In sentencesPart 1 · Das Verb auf Position 2 — the verb never moves

Ich fahre morgen nach Berlin.

I’m going to Berlin tomorrow.

fahren = to go (by vehicle), to drivenach Berlin = to Berlin

Ich habe morgen frei.

I have the day off tomorrow.

frei haben = to have time off

Morgen habe ich frei.

Tomorrow I have the day off.

morgen = tomorrowMorgen (1) · habe (2) · ich (3)

Jeden Tag jogge ich 10 km.

Every day I jog 10 km.

jeden Tag = every dayjoggen = to jog

Ich jogge jeden Tag 15 Kilometer.

I jog 15 kilometres every day.

der Kilometer · die Kilometer = kilometre

What the video shows: Ich habe morgen frei and Morgen habe ich frei mean the same. morgen rotates to the front, the verb habe stays in position 2, and the subject ich has to step behind it to position 3. Never *Morgen ich habe frei — that is English order.

In sentencesPart 2 · Ein Element, mehrere Wörter — one position, several words

Auf dem Balkon steht ein Tisch.

There’s a table on the balcony.

der Balkon · die Balkone = balconystehen = to standder Tisch · die Tische = table

In den Sommerferien steht man oft im Stau.

In the summer holidays you often get stuck in traffic.

die Sommerferien (nur Plural) = summer holidaysder Stau · die Staus = traffic jamim Stau stehen = to be stuck in traffic

Man steht in den Sommerferien oft im Stau.

People often get stuck in traffic in the summer holidays.

man = one, you, peopleoft = often

Morgen fliegt meine Schwester nach Thailand.

Tomorrow my sister is flying to Thailand.

fliegen = to flydie Schwester · die Schwestern = sister

The video's summary: the verb takes position 2. The subject takes position 1 or position 3. A time or place (morgen, jeden Tag, auf dem Balkon, in den Sommerferien) goes in position 1 or 3. And a “position” is a unit, not a word: auf dem Balkon (3 words) is position 1, meine Schwester (2 words) is position 3.

Notice Auf dem Balkon steht ein Tisch: the table comes after the verb, but it is still the subject (it is what stands) — so it is ein Tisch, never einen Tisch.

The step beyond the videoPosition 1 can even hold the object

bonus If position 1 is a free slot, anything can go there — including the object. The verb still stays second, and the subject moves behind it:

Ich esse den Kuchen. → Den Kuchen esse ich. (The cake — that's what I'm eating.)
Ich kenne den Film nicht. → Den Film kenne ich nicht. (That film I don't know.)

Germans do this for emphasis or to link to what was just said — the way English uses stress. Nothing is lost, because the ending still marks the roles. English cannot do this freely: The dog sees the man reordered is a different sentence.

The habitYour brain votes for the first noun

English sentences are overwhelmingly doer first, so your brain takes a shortcut: whatever noun I meet first is the one doing it. In German that is right most of the time — which is exactly what makes it dangerous. It fails only occasionally, so it never gets corrected.
This is a known, researched habit — VanPatten calls it the First Noun Principle. Learners who can recite the case rules still routinely misread sentences like Den Hund sieht die Katze as “the dog sees the cat”. Knowing the rule is not the problem; the habit fires first. The fix is to look at the form before you decide, until that becomes the reflex.

The fixTwo seconds, every time

When a German sentence starts with a noun or pronoun, check it before deciding anything:

Object signals — this is not the doer, the doer is further along: den Hund · einen Hund · meinen Bruder · keinen Hund · mich · dich · ihn · uns · euch
Subject signals — this is the doer: der Hund · ein Hund · mein Bruder (masculine nouns) · ich · du · er · wir

Feminine, neuter and plural (die Katze, das Kind, meine Eltern, sie, es) look identical as subject and object, so with those you read the other noun. That is why all the information sits in the masculine and in the pronouns.

If neither side is marked (Die Frau sieht das Kind), German reads it subject-first — and speakers avoid putting the object first in exactly those sentences.
